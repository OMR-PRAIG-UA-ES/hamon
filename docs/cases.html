Worked cases

Two things people do with an interlingua. Audit a corpus whose harmony is written down several times over, and carry an analysis out to every encoding that will take it. Both run as tours in the repository, and the output below is what they printed when this page was built.

Auditing a corpus — the DCML Choro Songbook

The Choro Songbook Corpus (Moss, Souza, Rohrmeier) stores the same music twice: a merged table with one row per chord onset, and a per-piece form grammar. Each chord is written three times over, as an absolute symbol, as a Roman numeral in the local key, and as a Harte label. Trusting it means asking two questions that are painful by hand and one line each once every encoding is in the same model.

Do the two representations agree chord for chord? And do the three encodings of each chord name the same harmony? The second one is the hard one: A7, V7 and A:7 are the same chord only once you know the key and each notation's rules.

Choro corpus audit complete.

Over the full corpus 292 of 295 pieces reproduce each other exactly, and both correctness audits pass at 100%. The three that diverge are the finding: cross-file, cross-encoding, easy to miss by hand.

The corpus is CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 and HAMON does not redistribute it: the run above used a local copy, and hamon datasets download choro fetches it from its authors. Everything HAMON adds is the exchange and validation layer; the data and its terms stay with DCMLab.

From a score with no analysis to every encoding

A Bach chorale out of music21's corpus: four voices, a key signature, and not one harmonic label in the file. The chain is analyse, carry, write — and the only part that is HAMON's is the carrying. music21 does the musicology; FlexOHR (DCMLab) is the object model at the far end. HAMON is what lets them be swapped without the harmony quietly degrading on the way.

The split in that table is clean because the input is a pure Roman analysis. The formats built for functional analysis carry every bit of it; the formats built for chord symbols keep no numerals at all, so for them the analysis is the loss. Run the same chain on a lead sheet and the table turns over, which is the argument the ICCCM'26 page makes at length.

The corpora HAMON reads

Both cases above lean on the same fact: the formats research actually ships in are readable without a bespoke parser per corpus. HAMON reads 22 formats and writes 13. The other 9 are read-only dialects that nothing writes back — each arrived with its own paper, its own table layout and its own parser.

Distant Listening Corpus  dcml            I V64 I6 V7/V V
Jazz Harmony Treebank     treebank        Dm7 G7 Cmaj7 A7 Dm7
Choro Songbook            choro           C G7 C G7 C
Key / Modulation (DDMAL)  key_modulation  I IV V I IV
DiLeMMa pitch array       dilemma         I V7/V V   (4 notes -> 3 harmonies)

22 formats in, 13 out. The other 9 are read-only dialects nothing writes back.

FlexOHR (DCMLab)          A7[of:ii] -> C(SPC(A)) dominant_seventh root_position
                                       P1 M3 P5 m7
                          back -> A major dom7   (the glyphs and [of:ii] stay behind)

Among them: the Distant Listening Corpus (Hentschel, Moss, Neuwirth, Rohrmeier), the Jazz Harmony Treebank (Harasim, Finkensiep et al.), the Choro Songbook above, DiLeMMa (Hentschel et al.) whose note-level pitch arrays collapse back to one label per harmony, and the DDMAL key/modulation set. The full list, with authors, licenses and download recipes, is the dataset manifest.
